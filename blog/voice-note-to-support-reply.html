How to Write a Support Reply With a Voice Note

Most bad support replies are written while annoyed. Recording what actually happened first, then letting the Support Script style draft the reply, separates your reaction from the answer the customer needs.

November 20, 2026 7 min read Elaine
Download on the App Store

Key takeaways

  • Replies written while annoyed read as defensive, even when the facts are right.
  • Record the story first — including the sentence you would never send.
  • Support Script gives the reply a standard shape; you supply the specifics and the policy.
  • A reply should contain only what you checked, what happens next, and when.

Support replies fail in two directions. The templated one answers a question the customer did not ask. The emotional one explains your day, defends the product, and buries the answer three paragraphs down.

Both are easier to avoid if you talk before you type. Two minutes out loud gets the frustration out of your system, and it makes you say what you actually know — which is the only thing the customer can use.

How to do it in VoiceNote

Step 1 — Talk the ticket through, unfiltered

Open VoiceNote and tap the mic. Read the ticket back in your own words, then say what you did and what you found. The free plan records up to two minutes per note; Prime extends a single recording to fifteen, which is plenty for a complicated case.

  • Say the annoyed version first. “This is the third time this week and the help centre says the opposite” is not going to the customer, but it tells you where the reply needs to be patient.
  • Say exactly what you checked. Which dashboard, which field, what it showed. Those facts become the only claims the reply is allowed to make.
  • Say what you are not sure about. “I think the refund goes back in three to five days, but I'd check” is the difference between a reply and a liability.
  • Say what the customer actually wants. It is often not the thing the ticket's first line asks for.

Step 2 — Apply Support Script

Open the note, tap AI Style Conversion and choose Support Script. The style produces a standard customer service reply: a calm, direct answer in a shape customers recognise.

The draft is a shape, not a decision. It does not know your refund policy, your SLA, or what you already promised on the phone, and it cannot see the account. Anything it states about your product needs checking before it is sent.

Step 3 — Cut it to three lines and one specific

Rewrite the draft until it holds only these parts, in this order:

  • What happened, in one sentence. Acknowledged, not narrated — “Your card was charged twice on the 14th” beats a paragraph about what the system usually does.
  • What you did or will do. Specific action, not reassurance: “I've refunded the duplicate charge” rather than “I'll make sure this is looked into”.
  • When they will see it. A timeframe you can defend, or an honest “I'll come back to you by Thursday”.
  • One human sentence. The line only you could write — and the only reason the reply does not read like a script.

Then remove every promise you cannot keep, every internal term the customer does not use, and every “as per our policy”. If the answer is no, say no in the first two lines and explain afterwards.

Why talking first works on support tickets

Anger is expensive to write with. When you type while irritated, the reply is polite in wording and defensive in structure: it explains before it answers, and it spends its words protecting the company.

Saying it out loud first spends that energy somewhere it costs nothing. The recording hears the version you would not send; the reply can be written from the facts alone.

It also helps with the second problem, which is knowing less than you think. Speaking forces you to name what you checked, and the gaps become obvious: you will hear yourself say “I assume” and know to go and look.

And it builds a record. Support teams get the same ticket for the sixth time because nobody captured the answer; a one-minute recording plus the reply is exactly the material a help-centre article needs.

A worked example

A made-up example. An agent records a duplicated-charge ticket before writing anything.

Spoken ticket notes

“Ticket from a customer who says she was charged twice. Honestly the third one of these this week and the help centre still says we never charge twice, which is why I'm annoyed. Okay. I checked the payments dashboard: two authorisations on the fourteenth, one at 09:12 and one at 09:14, both for 24 pounds, and the second one settled. So it is a real duplicate, not a pending authorisation — I refunded it at 10:05, transaction id ends 44. Refunds usually land in three to five working days but I don't want to promise that without checking, so I'll say up to five. She also asked why it happened. I don't know. The checkout retried after a timeout, probably, but I can't say that as a fact, so I'll keep it to what I know. What she actually wants is the money back, not an explanation.”

Support Script reply, after cutting

Hi Mia,

You were charged twice on the 14th: £24 at 09:12 and again at 09:14. The second charge went through, so it was a duplicate rather than a pending authorisation.

I refunded the second charge this morning — reference ends 44. It usually appears within five working days.

I can see the checkout retried, but I can't yet tell you exactly why, and I'd rather not guess. I've flagged it to the payments team so it doesn't happen to you again.

Sorry you had to chase this.

Sam

What changed: the frustration stayed in the recording, the reply leads with what happened, the timeframe is defensible, and the one uncertain answer is admitted rather than invented.

Before you send

  • Check every number, reference and date. A wrong transaction id creates a second ticket.
  • Read it as the customer. If the first two lines do not tell them whether the problem is solved, rewrite them.
  • Remove internal vocabulary. “Authorisation”, “settled” and ticket ids belong in your notes, not in the reply.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Sending the recording's tone. The point of the spoken version is to leave it behind.
  • Promising a timeline you did not verify. “Should be fine” is how an SLA breach starts.
  • Explaining before answering. Put the outcome in the first two lines; context comes after.
  • Using the draft unchanged. It cannot know your policy or the account, so unchecked claims are guesses.
  • Hiding a no. A refusal buried in paragraph four reads as a bait-and-switch.

Frequently asked questions

Can VoiceNote check the customer's account?

No. The draft is built from what you recorded, so anything about the account, the policy or the timeline has to come from you and your systems.

Can I turn one reply into a template for the team?

Yes — replace the customer-specific details with placeholders, keep the structure, and add a line saying when the template does not apply. That last line is what stops a template becoming a wrong answer.

How long can the ticket notes be?

Two minutes per note on the free plan, fifteen with Prime. For a complicated case, one note for the facts and one for what you are unsure about works well.

How is this different from a professional email?

A professional email is written for a colleague or a partner and can carry context and reasoning. A support reply is written for someone having a bad day: it leads with the outcome and keeps only what helps.

What if the same question keeps arriving?

That is a documentation problem, not a reply problem — write the answer up once and link it from the reply.

Which languages does VoiceNote support?

VoiceNote transcribes 46 languages. Write to the customer in their language if you can; style names in the app stay in English.

If you want the reply to sound like your team rather than any team, read it aloud once before sending — the same ear test works on support.
